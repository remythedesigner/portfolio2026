User research

A starting hypothesis

First interviews revealed something more precise. For most of the respondents, looking for weekend activities is often a source of stress because major events sell out quickly.

The problem

We assumed the main problem was choice overload in a buzzing city: smaller events struggle to find their audiences, and vice versa.

Design principles

Three decisions that shape the experience

01 Curation over volume

30 hand-picked, verified activities beat 300 unfiltered listings. The catalogue should be a quality promise, not just a suggestion.

02 Spontaneity first

Onboarding to booking confirmed should be under 2 minutes. Every tap that can be removed should be.

03 Human by design

From "Salut Jane 👋" to human-written activity descriptions, the app speaks with warmth and a real voice. In a category of generic platforms, tone of voice is the brand.

Features

The app in 5 key moments

1 Personalisation

An onboarding flow that gets straight to the point

Users reach a personalised home without a lengthy setup. They are mainly asked about their preferences, so that the activities suggested on the homepage suit them.

No blank slate, no friction before the first discovery.

2 Discovery

A home that curates, not just lists

A first-name greeting, the city weather, a curated featured activity with a live scarcity badge. Then several sections: team picks, personalised recommendations, and a geolocation radius.

No generic feed. Every section has a reason to be there and a reason to be different tomorrow.

3 Exploration

Explore the city, your way

Every category gets its own emoji pin on the map. A 🧘 for wellness, a 🎭 for culture, a 🍽 for food. The city becomes readable and diverse at a glance. A list toggle lets users compare activities side by side without losing the spatial context.

Filters were designed around real usage patterns from research: a rolling 7-day date strip (not just "tonight" or "this weekend")*, a category picker, a price range slider, a headcount picker, and a promo toggle. Every filter answers a question users actually asked.

Research insight

*13 / 17 participants needed date-specific filters.

4 Trust

The page where the decision happens

A swipeable photo gallery sets the scene before anything else. Below it: a rating, a metro stop, a duration, a cancellation policy, and a live scarcity count. The included/not-included and "Good to know" sections remove the last source of ambiguity before payment.

Reviews were intentionally not displayed at first but were gradually added to the app based on user feedback*.

Research insight

*14 / 17 cited missing reviews as their top friction.

5 Confirmation

A code or QR?

Venue professionals are diverse. Not all have a scanner. A 6-character code works with a tablet, a printed list, or just a pair of eyes. No equipment required, no friction at the door!

Research insight

Almost all users said they expected a QR code here, but none asked for one back after seeing the code and reading the note below.

User tests

Testing the prototypes

17 participants · 2 waves of 45-minute semi-structured interviews

Working alongside the founder, I ran two rounds of user testing across the project. We were not looking to confirm what we already thought but for where we were wrong.

One finding came up in nearly every session: users could not commit to a booking without reviews. The aggregate score told them how many people had gone. It told them nothing about whether they should.

In V2, we introduced the &joy team's own recommendations — written with conviction, the way a bookseller recommends a book. But testers explained they couldn't trust a review written by the people selling the experience…

14 / 17 Missing reviews (testing V1)

A score without comments was seen as invented. "21 reviews and I can't read them." The trust gap on the activity page was the sharpest finding of the study.

9 / 17 Biased editorial (testing V2)

"It's the &joy team saying it's good... Obviously, they won't say it's bad." Team reviews were read but not trusted.

6 Community

Trusted by the community, approved by the city

Ambassadors are not hired reviewers but selected users based on how well they know the city and their online reputation. The app gives them early access to new activities for free. In return, they write a detailed, signed review. The review is attached to their name and their reputation — that is what makes it readable, where the team's own voice was not.

In terms of pure design, ambassador reviews are visually highlighted and appear alongside standard user reviews, without replacing them. The mechanism goes further than just reviews: an activity that does not earn ambassador approval does not enter the catalogue. Ambassadors are not just the voice of the app. They are its quality filter.

I joined the &joy project to help with positioning. Seventeen interviews and dozens of iterations later, the final prototype was handed off to developers. I was glad to bring my expertise across the whole chain: product strategy, user research, visual identity, and interface design.
